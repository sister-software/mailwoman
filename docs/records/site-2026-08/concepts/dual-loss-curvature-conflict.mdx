# Dual-loss curvature conflict — when CRF is the aggressor

:::tip[No ML background needed]

If you are a software engineer without machine learning experience, start with the **[Beginner's companion](#beginners-companion--the-cooperative-vs-conflict-model)** below. It explains the problem without math.

:::

This page describes a failure of CE + CRF dual-loss training that appeared in nine of mailwoman's v0.5.0 attempts. The diagnostic technique applies to other dual-loss setups, and the fix is simple once the cause is known.

Read [the v0.4.0 ablation retrospective](https://github.com/sister-software/mailwoman/blob/main/docs/records/retrospectives/v0-4-0-ablation-campaign.mdx) and the [bisect-by-elimination blog post](pathname:///research/2026-05-24-bisect-by-elimination) first. They describe the failure pattern this page diagnoses.

## The failure fingerprint

Every recipe variant showed the same training curve. The variants covered different learning rates and hidden sizes, and each ran with and without class weights, per-token CRF normalization, and phrase-prior input features:

1. **Clean descent through warmup.** Loss drops monotonically as expected.
2. **Brief plateau near loss 0.41** (the deepest point any run reached).
3. **Sharp climb back to the starting magnitude** over the next 100-300 steps.

Validation macro-F1 followed the train loss curve. It peaked when loss bottomed and fell to roughly the random baseline as loss climbed. A lower learning rate moved the collapse later, but halving the LR delayed it by only ~1.3×. That response is too sub-linear to fit a learning rate that was too high.

The bisect ruled out learning rate, per-token CRF normalization (§1), class-weighted cross-entropy (§3), hidden size (h384 vs h256), and phrase-prior input features. The remaining suspects were the tokenizer and corpus pair, plus one we had not yet considered: the dual loss itself.

## The diagnostic technique

For a model trained on a sum of two loss terms (here CE + `λ`×CRF NLL), a cheap test shows which loss dominates the optimization right before divergence. Take a checkpoint from the step immediately before the climb. Run one forward pass and two backward passes, one with CE only and one with CRF only, then compare the gradient norms.

```python
# CE-only backward
ce_loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, num_labels), labels.view(-1), ...)
ce_loss.backward(retain_graph=True)
ce_norm = sum(p.grad.detach().pow(2).sum() for p in model.parameters() if p.grad is not None) ** 0.5

# CRF-only backward
model.zero_grad()
crf_loss = model.crf(emissions=logits, tags=labels.clamp(min=0), mask=crf_mask, reduction=crf_reduction)
crf_loss.backward()
crf_norm = sum(p.grad.detach().pow(2).sum() for p in model.parameters() if p.grad is not None) ** 0.5

ratio = crf_norm / max(ce_norm, 1e-12)
```

The probe runs in a few minutes against an existing checkpoint. It needs only two backward passes on stored weights, without retraining, train-loop instrumentation, or a special-mode flag.

## What the probe revealed

The probe sampled two checkpoints. One sat at the inflection point (loss settled at 0.63, about to climb) and one was deep in the climb (loss 1.92):

| Checkpoint           | Phase                   | `‖∇_CE‖` | `‖∇_CRF‖` | **ratio**       |
| -------------------- | ----------------------- | -------- | --------- | --------------- |
| v3-ablation step-500 | settled, about to climb | 7-17     | 149-275   | **median 16.2** |
| phrase-off step-1500 | deep in climb           | 4-5      | 30-46     | **median 8.0**  |

The probe produced two unexpected results:

1. **The CRF gradient is 8-20× larger than the CE gradient.** With `crf_loss_weight=0.05`, the effective CE:CRF contribution to the backward pass is roughly 1:0.8. The hand-tuned `crf_loss_weight` did not reduce CRF to the light regularization it was meant to provide.
2. **The ratio shrinks during divergence (16 → 8).** The CRF gradient stays at 30-45, so the CRF term is not collapsing. The CE gradient grows relative to it as the model moves away from the CE basin.

## The cooperative vs conflict regime model

The probe results fit an explanation of why dual-loss training works early and breaks late.

**Above loss ~0.41 (cooperative regime).** The model starts at high entropy, with random predictions and no structural knowledge. Both loss surfaces slope toward the same broad basin of less random predictions. CE improves per-token accuracy, and CRF improves transition-probability coherence. Both objectives move the optimizer in the same direction, and training descends directly.

**Below loss ~0.41 (conflict regime).** The model leaves the high-entropy basin and starts making fine-grained decisions between similar tags and structural patterns the CRF can score. CE still favors per-token accuracy. CRF favors transition-probability shapes that can push individual tokens toward tags that are structurally valid but locally wrong. On this data, the two objectives now point in opposing directions.

The optimizer follows the loss with the larger gradient. The CRF gradient is 16× the CE gradient, so the CRF term controls the update. The model moves away from the CE basin toward a CRF-preferred region, and CE loss climbs as a result.

The 0.41 boundary is specific to this data and architecture. Different corpora, label spaces, or model sizes would move it. The general structure of the failure is the finding that transfers.

## Why the standard repairs don't help

Three recipe changes that should address this failed in mailwoman's v0.5.0 attempts:

- **Lowering `crf_loss_weight`** from 0.1 to 0.05 produced the v0.4.0-shipped weights. Those weights still showed early signs of the same fingerprint: postcode regressed and full-parse exact match fell. The gradient-norm asymmetry is too large for the weight to compensate. At `crf_loss_weight=0.05`, CRF still contributes 0.05 × 16 = 0.8× CE in optimization.
- **Per-token CRF normalization** (§1) was meant to make CRF NLL magnitude comparable to per-token CE. The probe shows that the _gradient_ magnitudes still differ widely after the _loss_ magnitudes are normalized. Balanced loss magnitudes do not imply balanced gradient magnitudes.
- **Global gradient clipping** to norm 1.0 applies to the combined gradient after the dual-loss sum. Clipping preserves the _relative_ dominance, so CRF keeps its 16× share.

Scaling loss values does not correct asymmetric _curvature_. Below the cooperative-regime boundary, small parameter changes produce large CRF NLL gradients on this data. Multiplicative weighting cannot balance that against the gentler CE surface.

## The repair

Remove the CRF NLL loss term from training. Keep the CRF as an inference-time structural decoder with its frozen transition mask and Viterbi decoding.

The CRF's structural benefits come from the **transition mask**. Training the transition matrix through NLL does not provide them. Those benefits are the absence of orphan I-tags and of `Saint → Petersburg` clipping bugs from BIO-invalid spans. The mask is hand-encoded in `crf.py`. It forbids `O → I-locality` and every other BIO-invalid transition by setting those entries to `-inf` at initialization. The training-side NLL added a learned refinement on top of the mask, and that learned refinement is what conflicts with CE.

```python
# Before — the v0.4.0 train loop
loss = ce_loss + self.crf_loss_weight * crf_loss

# After — CE-only training, CRF stays at inference via frozen mask + Viterbi
if self.crf is not None and attention_mask is not None and self.crf_loss_weight > 0:
    crf_loss = self.crf(emissions=logits, tags=labels.clamp(min=0), mask=crf_mask, reduction=crf_reduction)
    loss = ce_loss + self.crf_loss_weight * crf_loss
else:
    loss = ce_loss
```

The `self.crf_loss_weight > 0` condition keeps the change backward-compatible. Existing recipes that ship with `crf_loss_weight: 0.05` still compute the CRF loss. The new behavior applies only when a recipe sets `crf_loss_weight: 0.0`. The change adds no config field, so two flags cannot disagree.

Inference is unchanged. The runtime still calls `model.crf.decode(emissions, mask)` to run Viterbi over the frozen mask. Output stays structurally valid, and training no longer has the opposing-curvature conflict.

## What to measure when validating the repair

A CE-only smoke run should meet these checks before the repair is accepted:

- **Loss stays stable past step 2000.** Every prior dual-loss run diverged by step 2000. The repair works if CE-only loss stays at or below its basin minimum past that step.
- **val_macro_F1 ≥ 0.35 at step 2000.** v0.4.0-shipped is 0.36, and h256-bisect peaked at 0.40 before diverging. A score of 0.35 means the run is stable and nearly as good as the best dual-loss checkpoint. Stability is the more important result.
- **Per-tag F1 trajectory.** Total macro_F1 can hide a collapse in one tag. If `venue` F1 drops from 0.39 to 0.05 while `house_number` climbs, CE-only has left a quality redistribution problem. This check costs no extra work because eval already computes the confusion matrices for per-tag F1.

If the smoke run passes those checks, promote it to a full 50K-step CE-only training run. Quality refinements such as class weights, source weights, and longer schedules can then be added. Under dual-loss they were unsafe because they amplified whichever loss already dominated.

## What this technique generalises to

The same diagnostic applies to any training setup that sums two loss terms with a hand-tuned weight and trains well before diverging sharply:

1. Take a checkpoint from the step immediately before divergence.
2. Compute the gradient norm of each loss with a separate backward pass.
3. Read the ratio. A ratio far from one means one loss dominates. A multiplier set on loss values may not produce the gradient balance you assumed.

The failure can go either way. The auxiliary loss can dominate, as in mailwoman's case, or the primary loss can dominate, which is rarer and looks like the auxiliary loss being ignored. The diagnostic and the repair are the same in both cases. If a loss term destabilizes training, remove it from training, and reintroduce it at inference if its structural contribution justifies the integration cost.

## Beginner's companion — the cooperative-vs-conflict model

This section explains the same problem without math or ML jargon. Start here if you want the debugging story before the technical details.

### The two voices

Mailwoman's training combines two scoring systems, called **Voice A** and **Voice B** here:

- **Voice A** (cross-entropy loss) asks: "How good are your guesses for each individual word? Did you tag '350' correctly as a house number? Did you tag 'NY' correctly as a region?"
- **Voice B** (CRF loss) asks: "How sensible is your overall pattern? Is your sequence of tags structurally valid? Does it look like a real address?"

A working address parser needs both per-word accuracy and sensible patterns. Training combined both voices and scaled Voice B down to 5% of Voice A's contribution, on the assumption that Voice B would act as a light structural correction.

### The cooperative regime

Picture two GPS devices on a foggy hill, each reporting which way is downhill.

At the top of the hill, the loss is high and the model is still random. Every direction leads down, so both devices point roughly the same way. The model's loss decreases steadily through the warmup phase.

### The conflict regime

Lower in the valley, the loss is lower and the model is better, and the terrain has more detail. The two devices start to disagree. Voice A puts the valley floor to the left, and Voice B puts it to the right.

The direction you walk then depends on **the device with the larger signal**. We measured this with a snapshot of the model taken before the climb started. Voice B's gradient was **16× larger** than Voice A's. Even after scaling to 5%, Voice B contributed roughly 80% as much as Voice A to the parameter updates.

Voice B pulled the model away from the basin Voice A was guiding it toward. Voice A's per-word accuracy score got worse, which appeared as the loss climbing back up.

### The fix

**Remove Voice B from training.** Keep it for inference, when the trained model parses addresses. At inference the frozen CRF mask enforces its structural rules, which forbid orphan tags and invalid BIO sequences. During training, Voice A guides the model alone.

The change is one configuration line: `crf_loss_weight = 0.0`. The structural guarantees come from the hand-encoded mask. Training the transition matrix added no strength to those guarantees and was the part that conflicted with Voice A.

### Three lessons that generalise

1. **Adding two weighted losses can fail badly.** Two loss functions can have very different gradient magnitudes even when their loss _values_ look comparable. Scaling the loss value does not balance their contributions to the optimizer.
2. **Run cheap diagnostics first.** The gradient-norm probe took 5 minutes and answered a question that more than a month of retraining experiments had not. Exhaust the diagnostics that need no GPU before a retrain.
3. **ML debugging resembles ordinary program debugging.** It uses the same steps: bisect, isolate, instrument, hypothesize, and test. The hard part is describing what happens inside the model precisely enough. After that, the bug is usually findable.
