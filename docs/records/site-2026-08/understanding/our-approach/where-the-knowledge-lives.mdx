# Where the knowledge lives

Every geocoder is a bet about where to put what it knows. Before you compare feature lists — that's
[How Mailwoman compares](../../concepts/how-mailwoman-compares.mdx) — it's worth comparing this one
decision, because almost everything else follows from it: the download size, the update cost, what
you can audit, and whether the thing can run on the machine in front of you.

## Three kinds of knowledge

A system that turns `30 rockefeller plaza, new york` into a structured, geocoded address needs
three different kinds of knowledge, and they have very different shapes:

1. **Grammar** — what address _structure_ looks like: that a number before a street name is
   probably a house number, that the thing after a comma is probably a locality, that "St" ends a
   street but starts "St Petersburg". This generalizes: the rules of the game are mostly stable
   even for streets nobody has written down yet.
2. **Spelling** — how raw text becomes units a model can read. Subwords, characters, bytes: the
   representational plumbing.
3. **The world** — which places actually exist, where they are, what they contain. Enormous,
   ambiguous ("Washington" is a state, dozens of cities, and thousands of streets), and it changes
   every month.

The systems you might be replacing differ less in _how much_ they know than in _where they keep
each kind_.

## Four answers to the same question

**libpostal fuses all three into one artifact.** Its classifier is a CRF whose features are, in
effect, gigantic per-token memory tables; its dictionaries (street types, directionals, unit
designators, in ~60 languages) and its place-name knowledge ship inside the same ~2 GB download as
the model. It works — libpostal set the standard for open-source parsing — but the fusion has a
price: updating _any_ ingredient means rebuilding and redistributing all of it, and nothing about
its knowledge is inspectable or swappable from the outside.

**Pelias puts the knowledge in an index.** The parser is thin (Pelias calls libpostal); the
intelligence lives in an Elasticsearch cluster — synonym files, analyzers, and query-time
heuristics over a many-gigabyte import of the planet. The dictionary became infrastructure: to
know what Pelias knows, you stand up the cluster and feed it. Operationally honest, operationally
heavy.

**LLM-era parsers put everything in parameters.** Systems in the AddrKG-LLM direction hand the
task to a large generative model, sometimes coupled to a knowledge graph. The model _is_ the
grammar, the dictionary, and a lossy copy of the atlas all at once. That buys flexibility and
costs everything else: billions of parameters, GPU serving, no character-offset guarantees for
the labels it emits, and confidence you can't calibrate. When it doesn't know a place, it can
invent one — a failure mode the other architectures structurally cannot have.

**Mailwoman's bet is the factoring itself.** Each kind of knowledge goes where it generalizes
best, and _only_ structure lives in parameters:

| Knowledge   | Where it lives                                                                                                          | Why there                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Grammar     | a ~39 MB encoder ([why neural](./why-a-neural-parser.mdx))                                                              | structure generalizes; a small model trained on hundreds of millions of addresses learns the game's rules, not its roster                                                   |
| Spelling    | a [SentencePiece subword vocabulary](../../concepts/tokenization.mdx) with byte fallback                                | learned from the address corpus itself; byte fallback means no input is unrepresentable (measured: 0.155% of pieces over a 684M-row feed fall back to bytes)                |
| The world   | swappable data files — the gazetteer, [FST evidence channels](../../concepts/fst-gazetteer-prior.mdx), curated lexicons | the atlas changes monthly and must not require retraining; it reaches the model as _graded evidence_, so an unknown place is a normal input, not an out-of-vocabulary error |
| Query idiom | small curated tables (brand aliases, regional variants)                                                                 | "Macca's" → McDonald's is human knowledge with a maintainer, not a statistical fact                                                                                         |

The model consults the world through evidence channels at inference time — "this span matches a
known locality surface, with this ambiguity class" — and learned during training how much to
trust that evidence against context. Memorization was deliberately kept _out_ of the parameters
and moved into files you can read, diff, replace, and license independently.

## What the factoring buys

**Deployability.** The parser plus its runtime data fits in a browser tab — the
[demo](https://mailwoman.sister.software/demo/) downloads the real model and runs it locally.
That is not a stunt; it's the direct consequence of not carrying the atlas inside the weights.
None of the systems above can do this, for the same reason in each case: their knowledge
placement makes the minimum artifact enormous.

**Update cost.** New Who's On First release? Rebuild the gazetteer artifacts; the model doesn't
move. New brand aliases? Edit a table. Retraining happens when the _grammar_ needs to improve,
which is rare and deliberate — not whenever the world changes, which is constantly.

**Auditability.** Because each store is separate, each is measurable on its own terms: the
vocabulary's utilization can be counted over the training feed, the evidence channels can be
ablated to show exactly what the gazetteer contributes, and the parser's confidence is
[calibrated](../../concepts/confidence-calibration.mdx) rather than asserted. A fused system can
only be evaluated end-to-end; a factored one can be interrogated a layer at a time.

**Honest failure.** When Mailwoman meets a place it has never heard of, the gazetteer offers no
evidence, the model parses on structure alone, and the confidence score says so. Nothing invents
a coordinate.

## The same principle, one level down

This article is about where knowledge is _stored_. Its sibling, [The knowledge
ladder](./the-knowledge-ladder.mdx), is about when knowledge is _applied_ — cheap structural
checks before expensive inference, inference before resolution. The two are the same instinct in
different dimensions: keep each kind of knowledge where it's cheapest to keep right.

If you came here from Pelias, [From Pelias to Mailwoman](./from-pelias-to-mailwoman.mdx) tells
the historical version of this story — how a parser that began as a libpostal replacement ended
up re-answering the placement question instead.
