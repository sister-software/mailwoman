# Where the knowledge lives

Every geocoder makes a choice about where to store what it knows.
[How Mailwoman compares](../../concepts/how-mailwoman-compares.mdx) compares feature lists.
This article compares that one storage decision, because almost everything else follows from it:
the download size, the update cost, what you can audit, and whether the system can run on the machine in front of you.

## Three kinds of knowledge

A system that turns `30 rockefeller plaza, new york` into a structured, geocoded address needs
three different kinds of knowledge, and they have very different shapes:

1. **Grammar** is what address _structure_ looks like.
   A number before a street name is probably a house number, the text after a comma is probably a locality,
   and "St" ends a street name but starts "St Petersburg".
   Grammar generalizes, because these rules stay mostly stable even for streets nobody has written down yet.
2. **Spelling** is how raw text becomes units a model can read, such as subwords, characters, or bytes.
3. **The world** is which places exist, where they are, and what they contain.
   This knowledge is enormous and ambiguous ("Washington" is a state, dozens of cities, and thousands of streets),
   and it changes every month.

The systems you might be replacing differ less in _how much_ they know than in _where they keep
each kind_.

## Four answers to the same question

**libpostal fuses all three into one artifact.** Its classifier is a CRF whose features are, in
effect, gigantic per-token memory tables. Its dictionaries (street types, directionals, and unit
designators in ~60 languages) and its place-name knowledge ship inside the same ~2 GB download as
the model. libpostal works, and it set the standard for open-source parsing, but the fusion has a
price. Updating _any_ ingredient means rebuilding and redistributing all of it, and none of
its knowledge can be inspected or swapped from the outside.

**Pelias puts the knowledge in an index.** The parser is thin, because Pelias calls libpostal. The
rest of the knowledge lives in an Elasticsearch cluster as synonym files, analyzers, and query-time
heuristics over a several-gigabyte import of the planet. The dictionary became infrastructure. To
know what Pelias knows, you stand up the cluster and feed it. That is straightforward to operate
but heavy.

**LLM-era parsers put everything in parameters.** Systems in the AddrKG-LLM direction hand the
task to a large generative model, sometimes coupled to a knowledge graph. The model holds the
grammar, the dictionary, and a lossy copy of the atlas all at once. That design gains flexibility
and pays for it everywhere else. It needs billions of parameters and GPU serving, it gives no
character-offset guarantees for the labels it emits, and its confidence cannot be calibrated. When
it doesn't know a place, it can invent one, which the other architectures structurally cannot do.

**Mailwoman's choice is the factoring itself.** Each kind of knowledge goes where it generalizes
best, and _only_ structure lives in parameters:

| Knowledge   | Where it lives                                                                                                          | Why there                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Grammar     | a ~39 MB encoder ([why neural](./why-a-neural-parser.mdx))                                                              | structure generalizes; a small model trained on hundreds of millions of addresses learns the game's rules rather than its roster                                                   |
| Spelling    | a [SentencePiece subword vocabulary](../../concepts/tokenization.mdx) with byte fallback                                | learned from the address corpus itself; byte fallback means no input is unrepresentable (measured: 0.155% of pieces over a 684M-row feed fall back to bytes)                       |
| The world   | swappable data files — the gazetteer, [FST evidence channels](../../concepts/fst-gazetteer-prior.mdx), curated lexicons | the atlas changes monthly and must not require retraining; it reaches the model as _graded evidence_, so an unknown place is a normal input rather than an out-of-vocabulary error |
| Query idiom | small curated tables (brand aliases, regional variants)                                                                 | "Macca's" → McDonald's is human knowledge with a maintainer rather than a statistical fact                                                                                         |

At inference time, the model consults the world through evidence channels, which report that a
span matches a known locality surface with a given ambiguity class. During training, the model
learned how much to trust that evidence against context. Memorization was deliberately kept _out_
of the parameters and moved into files you can read, diff, replace, and license independently.

## What the factoring provides

**Deployability.** The parser and its runtime data fit in a browser tab. The
[demo](https://mailwoman.ai/demo/) downloads the real model and runs it locally.
This follows directly from keeping the atlas out of the weights.
None of the systems above can do this, for the same reason in each case: where they store their
knowledge makes the minimum artifact enormous.

**Update cost.** For a new Who's On First release, you rebuild the gazetteer artifacts and leave
the model unchanged. For new brand aliases, you edit a table. Retraining happens only when the
_grammar_ needs to improve, which is rare and deliberate. The world changes constantly, and none of
those changes require retraining.

**Auditability.** Because each store is separate, each is measurable on its own terms. The
vocabulary's utilization can be counted over the training feed, the evidence channels can be
ablated to show exactly what the gazetteer contributes, and the parser's confidence is
[calibrated](../../concepts/confidence-calibration.mdx) rather than asserted. A fused system can
only be evaluated end-to-end, but a factored one can be examined a layer at a time.

**Visible failure.** When Mailwoman meets a place it has never heard of, the gazetteer offers no
evidence, the model parses on structure alone, and the confidence score reflects that. No stage
invents a coordinate.

## The same principle, one level down

This article is about where knowledge is _stored_. Its sibling, [The knowledge
ladder](./the-knowledge-ladder.mdx), is about when knowledge is _applied_: cheap structural
checks come before expensive inference, and inference comes before resolution. Both articles
apply the same principle to different dimensions, which is to keep each kind of knowledge where
it is cheapest to keep correct.

If you came here from Pelias, [From Pelias to Mailwoman](./from-pelias-to-mailwoman.mdx) tells
the historical version of this story. It describes how a parser that began as a libpostal
replacement ended up answering the storage question differently.
