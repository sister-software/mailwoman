# Postcodes and ZIP Codes

## The question

The postcode looks like the easiest part of an address to work with. It is short, machine-readable and
fixed in shape, and nearly every web form asks for it. Yet geocoding by postcode produces some of the
least accurate coordinates. This page explains why.

## The analog

A postcode is a sorting instruction. It encodes a path through a postal network: which regional
facility, which delivery office, which carrier route. It is written for the sorting machine and the
carrier, and it encodes what they need in the order they need it.

The US structure runs from east to west. The first digit is "a general geographic area of the
nation, '0' in the east, moving to '9' in the west". The next two digits are regional areas, and the
final two are specific post offices ([USPS](https://facts.usps.com/decoding-the-zip-code/)). The five
digits therefore end at a post office instead of at a building. The `+4` extension identifies something
smaller, and the Postal Service's own list of what it can identify is informative: "a side of a
street between intersections, both sides of a street between intersections, a building, a floor or
group of floors in a building, a firm within a building, a span of boxes on a rural route, or a group
of Post Office boxes to which a single USPS employee makes delivery"
([Domestic Mail Manual 602](https://pe.usps.com/text/dmm300/602.htm)). Two further digits, added in
1991, extend the code to the individual delivery point.

Every item on that list is a unit of delivery. None of them is a geographic shape.

## The same idea, five ways

Other postal services solve the same problem with different alphabets and different levels of detail.

The UK splits the code into an outward half and an inward half, and the hierarchy has countable levels:
124 postcode areas, 3,118 districts, 12,463 sectors, and roughly 1.79 million live unit postcodes. A
small-user unit postcode covers about fifteen addresses and never more than a hundred
([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/methodology/geography/ukgeographies/postalgeography)). At that level of
detail a UK postcode is close to a point, which is why `SW1A 1AA` behaves like an address in a geocoder
and `90210` does not.

Ireland went further. An Eircode has seven characters: a three-character routing key that identifies
the post town, then a four-character unique identifier that "is unique to an address and its geographic
location" ([Eircode](https://www.eircode.ie/what-is-eircode)). Each code identifies one delivery point
without interpolation. Before the system launched in 2015, Irish addresses had no postcode at all.

Canada alternates letters and digits (`K1A 0B1`): a forward sortation area, then a local delivery
unit. The Netherlands uses four digits and two letters. France uses five digits whose first two are the
département. As a result, `06130` and `6130` are different places, and a system that stores postcodes
as integers loses the difference.

## Why the polygon is somebody's approximation

Drawing a postcode as a shape is a habit that comes from a real file, and that file describes its own
limits. The US Census Bureau created ZIP Code Tabulation Areas because "ZIP Codes are stored as one component of
discrete addresses tied to delivery points, including specific housing unit locations. The result is
a point-based dataset unsuitable for mapping …"
([Census](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/geography/guidance/geo-areas/zctas.html)). The
Bureau generalized census blocks into areas so that statistics keyed by ZIP Code could be mapped at
all. The Bureau constructed these areas, and they are not Postal Service boundaries. The same page states that the Postal Service "assigns ZIP Code ranges to regional post offices, which in turn assign ZIP
Codes to delivery routes."

The UK states the same limitation from the other direction: "postcode boundaries mostly do not align
with other geographic boundaries," and they move as addresses are built and mail volumes change
([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/methodology/geography/ukgeographies/postalgeography)).

The errors therefore come from how postcodes are defined, even when the data is correct. A rural code covers a
route, and its centroid falls wherever the route's addresses average out. A box-only code centers on
the post office instead of on the homes of the people who rent the boxes. A code that crosses a town
line belongs to two municipalities and fits neither of their outlines.

## What this costs a parser

This has two consequences for a parser, and they pull in opposite directions.

First, a postcode is strong evidence about a region and weak evidence about a point. Used as a
constraint on where to look, it is valuable. Used as the answer, it produces coordinates whose error
does not show in the output.

Second, a postcode shape does not identify a single country. Five digits match the US pattern, the
French pattern and part of the German one, so the country of a bare `75001` is ambiguous.
Mailwoman's kind classifier reports that it received only a postcode.
[Handle PO boxes and other edge kinds](../../how-to/handle-po-boxes-and-edge-kinds.mdx) has a measured
table of where five bare postcodes resolved without a country scope. Four of them are US codes, and
none of those four resolved to the US. That page also shows the fix, which is to scope the query by
country, and the remaining problem, which is that scoping turns some wrong answers into no answer.
