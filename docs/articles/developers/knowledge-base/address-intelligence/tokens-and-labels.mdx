# Tokens and labels

## The question

The parser reports spans of your input string: characters 35 to 45 are a locality. Underneath, it is
making one decision per piece of text, and the pieces are not words — `analytics` arrives at the
model as `▁Ana ly t ic s`. Two questions follow from that. Why fragments rather than words? And how
do five separate per-piece decisions add up to one span you can act on?

## The analog

You have written the word-list version of this. A set of known street suffixes, a set of state
abbreviations, a table of city names — and a tokenizer that splits on whitespace so the tokens line
up with the table keys.

It works until it meets a word the table has never seen, and then it has nothing at all to say. Not a
weaker opinion: no opinion, because the lookup either hits or misses. `Pennsylvana`, typed by a
person in a hurry, is as unknown to a word table as `Xzqjrt`. And the table itself has no natural
size — every new country, every new spelling, every business name is another key, and a table with
millions of keys is not a table any more, it is the corpus.

The fix is the printer's type case rather than the dictionary. Keep a fixed inventory of fragments,
and spell every word out of them. Rare words cost more fragments; unseen words still get spelled.
Nothing is unrepresentable.

## Pieces, not words

Mailwoman spells input with [SentencePiece](https://github.com/google/sentencepiece), an
open-source subword tokenizer. The inventory holds 73,143 pieces, chosen by a unigram language-model
fit over training text, and the `▁` prefix marks a piece that begins a new word. Common words are one
piece; `▁Washington` is a single unit. Uncommon ones fragment, and the fragmentation carries
information — a word that splits into five pieces is a word the training text did not see much of.

Two properties matter downstream.

**The inventory is closed and small.** A model's output layer and its embedding table are both sized
by the inventory, so bounding it at 73,143 is what keeps the shipped model a 39.4 MB file rather than
a multi-gigabyte one.

**Nothing is out of vocabulary.** When a character is not covered by any piece, the tokenizer falls
back to encoding it byte by byte. A Cyrillic street name or a stray emoji produces pieces rather than
a failure. Those pieces carry no learned meaning — byte fallback is what stops the pipeline from
crashing, and it is not coverage of the script. [What the model cannot do](./what-the-model-cannot-do.mdx)
draws that line.

## Pieces are not what you get back

The piece boundaries are an implementation detail of the model, and the parse result never exposes
them. Each piece carries the character range it came from, reported by the tokenizer itself rather
than reconstructed afterward, and the decoder projects labels back onto those ranges. `▁Ana ly t ic s`
covers characters 13 through 22 of the input, so a venue span that runs from `▁Beacon` to `s` is
reported as characters 0 through 22 — one span, in your coordinate system.

Keep the distinction in mind when you read a confidence score, because the score is per piece before
it is per span. A five-piece word contributes five numbers that get aggregated into the one you
see.

There is one place the piece-versus-word distinction becomes visible in behavior. Because pieces are
labeled independently, the five pieces of one word can disagree — three of them venue, two of them
street — which is a reading no person would produce and no consumer wants. After decoding, the
parser groups pieces back into their `▁`-delimited words and, for any word whose pieces disagree
about which kind of component they belong to, holds a confidence-weighted vote and applies the winner
across the whole word. It runs by default, and it operates strictly inside a word: `Saint Petersburg`
is two words and is never merged by this pass.

## BIO: the bridge from "matched" to "labeled"

**The rule-world version:** a regular expression returns a span directly. It matched from here to
there, and the span is the match.

**What happens here:** the model produces one label per piece, and a label alone cannot express a
boundary. If every piece of `Saint Petersburg, FL` were labeled just `locality`, `locality`,
`region`, there would be no way to tell one two-word town from two one-word towns sitting side by
side.

BIO labeling is the standard fix, and it is older than any of this — it is what named-entity
recognition has used since the 1990s. Each label carries a position as well as a type:

- `B-locality` — a locality span **begins** at this piece.
- `I-locality` — this piece is **inside** the locality span already open.
- `O` — this piece is **outside** every component.

So `Saint Petersburg, FL` labels as `B-locality`, `I-locality`, `O`, `B-region`, and the span
reconstruction is a single walk: open at `B-`, extend at a matching `I-`, close at anything else. Two
adjacent towns would read `B-locality`, `B-locality`, and the second `B-` is the boundary.

The scheme has one structural weakness, and it is the reason the decoder exists: `I-locality` with no
`B-locality` before it is not a reading of anything. A per-piece argmax will produce that sequence
whenever the model is unsure about the first word and confident about the second, and the standard
symptom is a town losing its first word. [Decoding and the best path](./decoding-and-viterbi.mdx) is
where that gets ruled out.

## Three counts, and why they differ

Three numbers get quoted around the label space, and they answer different questions.

The **schema** defines 25 component tags — everything a parse result is allowed to carry, including
tags for Japanese address structure and tags no shipped model produces. At two labels per tag plus
one shared `O`, that is a capacity of 51 labels.

The **shipped model's head** is fixed at training time and carries 33 labels over 16 tags. That is
the alphabet this model can write in. Nine tags in the schema are outside it: seven belong to a
separate Japanese head that is not shipped, and two are carried for annotation and formatting rather
than produced by the parser.

The gap between the two is not a defect to work around, and it is not post-processing that fills in
later. A tag outside the head never appears in a parse from this model.
[Component tags](../../reference/component-tags.mdx) lists all 25 with meanings and examples, and
states per model which are reachable.

## What it costs

**Boundaries are implicit, so they inherit label errors.** The model has no separate boundary head:
a span begins where a `B-` label lands. That means it cannot hold a confident view of where a street
ends while remaining unsure what the street is — boundary confusion is downstream of label confusion,
always. When a house number gets absorbed into a street name, the fix is evidence about the label,
not a boundary rule.

**Fragmentation is uneven across languages.** A language whose text is thin in the training corpus
spells into more pieces per word, and more pieces per word means a longer sequence, more chances for
two pieces of one word to disagree, and less of the fixed 128-piece input window left for the rest of
the address. The cost of thin coverage shows up here first, before it shows up as a wrong label.

**The vote is per word, not per name.** The consistency pass keeps one word internally coherent and
deliberately stops there. Multi-word names are left to the decoder and the priors, because a rule
that forced agreement across a whitespace boundary would merge a locality into the street in front of
it.
