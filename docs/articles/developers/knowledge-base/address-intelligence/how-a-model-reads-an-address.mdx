# How a model reads an address

## The question

Hand the parser this, exactly as a user typed it into a phone:

```
beacon ridge analytics 800 f st nw washington dc 20004
```

No commas. No capitals. A company name sitting in front of the address. A street whose entire name is
one letter. Six fields come back, and all six are right. What happened in between?

This page walks that one line through the parser end to end. Every stage gets its rule-world name
before its statistical one, because every stage is doing a job you have written by hand at some
point — a lookup table, a regular expression, a tie-break. The machinery is those same jobs, done
with scores instead of verdicts.

## The analog

Give the line to a person with a highlighter and ask them to mark it up. They will draw four
stretches: the company, the number, the street, and the town-plus-state-plus-postcode tail. They will
do it in one pass with the whole line in view, because the reason `dc` is a state is that
`washington` sits to its left and five digits sit to its right — nobody reads `dc` in isolation and
then moves on. And when they finish, they will glance back over the marking to check it reads: a
"this continues the town" stroke with no "the town starts here" stroke before it is a slip of the
pen, not a reading of the line.

That is the whole design. Four moves: cut the line into pieces, score every possible mark on every
piece, add what a place list already knows, and pick the marking that reads.

## One: cut the line into pieces

**The rule-world version:** split on spaces and punctuation, and you have words.

**What happens here:** the pieces are smaller than words. `analytics` arrives at the model as five of
them:

```
▁Ana  ly  t  ic  s
```

The `▁` marks a piece that starts a new word. The pieces come from a fixed inventory of 73,143 of
them, built once from training text, and any string at all can be spelled out of that inventory —
which is the property a word list does not have. A word list meets `Analytics` for the first time and
has nothing to say; a piece inventory spells it out of pieces it has seen thousands of times.

Two things are worth carrying out of this stage. Each piece records where it came from in your
original string, so nothing downstream ever needs to know that pieces exist — you get character
offsets into the text you passed in. And the text the model sees is not byte-for-byte the text you
typed: a fully lowercase ASCII input is restored to mixed case first, because that is the register
the training corpus was labeled in. `beacon` reaches the model as `Beacon`.
[Tokens and labels](./tokens-and-labels.mdx) covers both properly.

## Two: score every mark on every piece

**The rule-world version:** a pattern that fires or does not. `if (/^\d{5}$/.test(token))` and you
have a postcode.

**What happens here:** nothing fires. Every piece receives a score for every one of the 33 available
marks, and the marks compete. `▁2` gets a score for "postcode starts here", a score for "house number
starts here", a score for "outside every component", and thirty more.

The scores are computed with the entire line visible at once. That is the part a per-token rule
cannot reach. `F` is scored as the beginning of a street name because `800` sits to its left and a
locality-shaped word, a two-letter uppercase token and a five-digit number sit to its right — the
same evidence the person with the highlighter used, and for the same reason. Nothing about the
letter `F` on its own suggests a street.

## Three: add what the place list already knows

**The rule-world version:** the gazetteer lookup you have written before. Take the token, check it
against a list of place names, and if it is in there, it is a place.

**What happens here:** the same lookup, made additive instead of decisive. `Washington` matches the
place index, so the pieces it covers get a bonus on the locality marks and a penalty on street, house
number and venue. The bonus is capped, and it is withheld entirely where a match sits in
street-shaped surroundings, so a street named after a town stays a street:
`800 Washington Ave, Portland, OR` parses the same with the index wired and without it. In the run
above, the place index was the one prior that found anything to say.
[The gazetteer prior](./the-gazetteer-prior.mdx) is the full treatment.

## Four: pick the marking that reads

**The rule-world version:** run your classifiers, then reconcile the mess afterward with tie-break
heuristics — prefer the longer span, drop the continuation that has no beginning, and so on.

**What happens here:** whole readings are scored rather than repaired. A table built once from the
label list says which mark may follow which — "continues the street" is legal after "street starts
here" and after itself, and after nothing else — and the decoder searches for the highest-scoring
reading that stays inside those rules. A continuation with no beginning is not fixed up after the
fact; it never appears, because no legal reading contains one.
[Decoding and the best path](./decoding-and-viterbi.mdx) has the mechanism.

Here is the reading that came out for our line, captured from the parse itself, one row per piece:

`B-` marks where a span begins and `I-` marks a piece that continues it, which is how a per-piece
decision becomes a span. Five pieces of `Analytics` carry five separate labels that together mean one
thing.

## What comes out

Walk that column, open a span at each `B-`, extend it at each matching `I-`, and close it at anything
else. Then nest the spans by geographic containment, and you have the result:

```
region        = "DC"                      46..48   0.876
locality      = "Washington"              35..45   0.921
street        = "F ST NW"                 27..34   0.892
venue         = "Beacon Ridge Analytics"   0..22   0.897
house_number  = "800"                     23..26   0.891
postcode      = "20004"                   49..54   0.905
```

The offsets point into the string you passed in, not into the case-restored copy the model read. The
values are the repaired forms — `F ST NW` rather than `f st nw` — which is why keeping your own copy
of the raw input is worth the line of code.

## What it costs

**A wrong field does not name its own stage.** Four things can produce `street="Washington"`: the
pieces split somewhere unhelpful, the scores were wrong, the place index missed, or the best legal
reading was a bad one. The four are not separable from the output alone, which is why
[Report a parsing bug](../../how-to/report-a-parsing-bug.mdx) asks for the input string rather than a
description of the symptom.

**Nothing in the chain abstains.** Every stage produces scores, and scores always have a maximum, so
there is always an answer. Hand it a line that is not an address and you get an address-shaped
reading of it. [What the model cannot do](./what-the-model-cannot-do.mdx) is the list of places that
matters.

**The number is narrower than the decision.** The score on each span reports how sure the model was
about that label on its own, before the place index was added in. So a span the place index corrected
can come back carrying a lower number than the wrong reading it replaced —
[The gazetteer prior](./the-gazetteer-prior.mdx#what-it-costs) has the measurement. If you plan to
route on the number, read [Calibration and confidence](./calibration-and-confidence.mdx) before you
pick a threshold.
