# The gazetteer prior

## The question

You have a list of every place name in the world, with a type attached to each. Why does the parser
not read the input, check each token against the list, and label the hits?

The short answer is that the list is right about which strings name places and wrong about which
strings are naming a place here. This page is about what to do with a place list once you accept
that: keep it, keep consulting it, and stop letting it decide.

## The analog

The gazetteer lookup is the first thing anyone writes. Tokenize the input, take each token, probe a
table of place names, and if the token is in there, it is a place.

It is a good instinct and it works on a large fraction of real input. Then it meets `Park Avenue`,
and `Park` names a place somewhere, so the street becomes a town. It meets `Paris, TX` and has no
basis for preferring the Texas one to the French one, because nothing in the table is about the `TX`
sitting next to it. It meets `Buffalo Health Clinic, Buffalo` and finds `Buffalo` twice, with nothing
to say about which one is the business and which one is the town.

Every one of those failures has the same shape. The lookup answers "is this string ever a place
name", and the question in front of you is "is this string being used as a place name here". The
first question is about the world, the second is about the sentence, and the table only holds
answers to the first.

So the fix is not a better table. It is to demote the table's answer from a verdict to a piece of
evidence, and to combine it with evidence about the sentence — which is exactly what the per-piece
scores already are.

## The mechanism

Before the model runs, the parser walks the input against a place index and collects every match.
For each match it produces a small set of adjustments to the per-piece scores:

- **A bonus on the matching component labels.** A locality match adds to `B-locality` and
  `I-locality` on the pieces it covers.
- **A penalty on the competing labels.** The same match subtracts from street, house number and
  venue on those same pieces, because a span that names a known town is evidence against those
  readings, not merely evidence for its own.

Those adjustments are added to the scores the model produced, and the sum is what the decoder reads.
That pattern has a name — shallow fusion, borrowed from speech recognition, where an acoustic model's
scores get a language model's scores added to them at decode time rather than being replaced by them.
The point of adding rather than substituting is that both opinions survive: the model contributes
what the sentence looks like, the index contributes what the world contains, and neither one is
consulted only when the other fails.

The bonus is scaled by how prominent the matched place is and capped at three points of score. That
cap is the whole safety property. Where the model's own top two readings are far apart, three points
does not close the gap and the model's reading survives; where they are close, three points is
decisive. The index gets a vote in proportion to how little the model had to say.

Three details are worth knowing because they explain behavior you can observe.

**Match length changes how hard the penalty bites, not how large the bonus is.** Length here is
counted in words. A one-word match is weak evidence, because surnames, common words and street heads
are place names somewhere, and a match spanning several words is stronger, because coincidence gets
less likely with every word. The default response is to discount the penalty on the competing
readings for a short match while leaving its bonus at full strength. That asymmetry is the point: a
bare `Hollywood` is a one-word match, and the full bonus is what makes it come back a locality —
discounting it would break the case the index is most needed for. What a wrong one-word match does
damage with is the penalty, so the penalty is the half that gets softened.

**A match in street-shaped surroundings gets no bonus at all.** When a matched name sits where a
street name sits — a street-type word beside it, or a house number to its left — the runtime pipeline
withholds the positive bonus entirely and keeps only the suppression. A street named after a town is
therefore not pulled toward the town by the index: `800 Washington Ave, Portland, OR` and
`123 Madison Ave, New York, NY 10016` both parse identically with the index wired and without it. The
suppression side is never withheld, because the index is used as evidence for a reading and against
its competitors, never as a penalty on a reading it has no opinion about.

**One span can match several kinds of place at once**, and the index does not choose between them.
`New York` is both a city and a state, and both entries are live. Each contributes a bonus to its own
component labels on the same pieces, and which one wins is settled downstream by the decoder reading
the rest of the line.

## What it can and cannot do

**It cannot resolve.** The index reports that a span could be a place of some type. It does not pick
which place, and it produces no coordinate. That is the resolver's job, and it happens after the
parse — see [Gazetteers](../geocoding/gazetteers.mdx) for the database and
[The two architectures](../geocoding/the-two-architectures.mdx) for where the two jobs sit.

**It speaks four component types.** A match only turns into a bonus when its place type is one the
model has labels for: country, region, locality, postcode. The index itself holds more than that —
querying `Boston` returns neighborhood entries alongside the locality — and every entry outside those
four contributes nothing, because there is no label for the bonus to land on.

**It carries administrative names, not streets and not venues.** A gazetteer of specific street names
would be several million more entries and is not what ships. There is a separate, much smaller
vocabulary of generic street-type words — `avenue`, `rue`, `straße` — and that one is a different
mechanism from this page's, with a different wiring status. Venue names are open-ended and are not in
any index, which matters more than it sounds: the hardest ambiguity in address parsing is a venue
named after a place, and that is the case the index has the least to say about.

**It cannot override a confident model.** By construction: the bonus is capped and the model's scores
are not. If the parse is wrong in a way a place-name lookup would plainly fix, the index is
not the thing that will fix it.

**It is a prior, not a stage, so its absence is silent.** The runtime pipeline loads the index from
the weights package on its first call and passes it to every parse. A classifier you construct and
call directly receives nothing, and the parse still succeeds — it is worse, without announcing it,
on exactly the inputs the index exists for. A bare `Hollywood` comes back as a street without it and
a locality with it.
[Library API](../../reference/library-api.mdx#wiring-the-gazetteer-index-on-a-bare-call) has the
wiring and the measured difference.

## What it costs

**The index is the second-largest file in the weights package, after the model itself.** 21.8 MB
against 39.4 MB, listed with everything else on [Footprints](../../reference/footprints.mdx). The
design trades build time for query time: enumerating every place name into a walkable structure is
done once, offline, so a parse only ever walks it. That is what lets the same index ship to a browser
instead of living behind a lookup service.

**A correction from the index does not raise the reported confidence.** The score attached to each
span reports the model's own opinion of the label that was chosen, computed before the index was
added in. So when the index flips a reading from wrong to right, the number on the corrected span is
the number the model gave that reading on its own — which is low, because the model preferred
something else. Measured on a bare `Hollywood`: 0.691 as a street without the index, 0.264 as a
locality with it. The label improved and the number fell. If you threshold on confidence, that is a
class of correct answer your threshold will route to review, and
[Calibration and confidence](./calibration-and-confidence.mdx) is where to take it.

**Coverage of the index is coverage of the gazetteer.** A town missing from the open data is a town
the prior cannot help with, and the failure looks like the model being unsure rather than like a
missing record.

**On the venue-versus-place case it changes nothing, and that case is still wrong.**
`Riverside Garden Center, Boston` reads as a single locality covering the whole leading phrase, with
`Boston` dropped from the result entirely — identical output with the index wired and without it.
`Riverside` and `Boston` are both in the index; neither match rescues the parse, because separating a
business name from the town after it is a judgment about how the words are being used, and the index
has no vocabulary for the business half. This is the shape of failure to expect from a prior that
supplies world knowledge rather than sentence structure.
