# Compare

These pages describe what other geocoding systems are built to do, in their own published words, so
you can tell which shape of tool your problem wants. Each one ends with a section on when to choose
that tool over this one, and each of those sections is written to be taken seriously rather than
survived.

What you will not find here is an accuracy comparison. Running someone else's service under terms we
did not write, on a query mix we chose, and publishing a score on their behalf is not a measurement
we would trust from anyone else. [Benchmarks](../benchmarks/index.mdx) is where numbers live, and
every number there is about our own output.

## I am evaluating a hosted API

A service you call over HTTPS with no data to download and no index to build. The trade is a per-
request relationship, a component vocabulary that is theirs rather than yours, and a query that
leaves your infrastructure.

[Google Maps Platform](./google-maps.mdx)

## I am evaluating a self-hosted engine

A geocoder you run, over OpenStreetMap data, with the queries staying on your network. The trade is
a provisioning and operations commitment that does not end at install.

[Self-hosted Nominatim](./self-hosted-nominatim.mdx)

## I am evaluating the open-source geocoding stack

Pelias and libpostal are the two pieces most teams meet when they build a geocoder rather than buy
one — a modular search-engine geocoder, and the address parser that a great deal of the field is
built on.

[Pelias and libpostal](./pelias-and-libpostal.mdx)

## The one distinction underneath all three

Two designs answer the geocoding question, and which one fits depends on what your input looks like
rather than on any feature table.

A **search-engine geocoder** indexes every place as a document and ranks your whole string against
the index. It is strong on partial input, landmarks, misspellings and search boxes, and it wants the
index resident.

A **label-then-look-up geocoder** labels the spans of your string first — house number, street,
locality, postcode — and then runs one constrained lookup per span. It returns a decomposition rather
than a match, and the constraint is what lets the reference data stay on disk.

Mailwoman is the second. Nominatim, Photon and Pelias are the first.
[The two architectures](../../developers/knowledge-base/geocoding/the-two-architectures.mdx) works
through what each design is good at and what each one costs, with the published hardware figures and
dated citations. Read it before the pages above if you have not settled that question, because it
decides more than any of them.
