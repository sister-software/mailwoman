# How Mailwoman locates an address

An address line can identify a place without identifying a building point. Mailwoman first parses the line into components. A geographic resolver can then match those components to place records and, when suitable data exists, to an address-point or street record. The returned coordinate describes the selected geographic evidence. Its position on a map doesn't by itself measure its error against the real destination.

## A place name needs an identity

The parser can label text as `locality`, `region`, or `postcode` without opening a geographic database. A resolver asks a place index which records identify those names. It can use a supplied country, a postcode, a parent place, or a caller-supplied proximity bias to scope and rank the candidates. The [parsing explanation](./parsing.mdx) describes why the parser's optional place-name matcher is a separate signal from this lookup.

The published `geocodeAddress` path supplies the parsed tree and options to `resolver.resolveTree`. The resolver can attach a place identity, coordinates, alternatives, and ancestry to nodes. The geocode extractor then projects that resolved tree into one `GeocodeResult`. Its `candidates` array can retain a selected place and distinct alternatives. A candidate's ranking score compares possibilities within that answer. It isn't a measure of distance from a verified address.

The place index and the address-point layers answer different questions. A gazetteer record can identify a city or administrative area and supply a representative point. An address-point row can associate a street and number with its own recorded coordinate. An interpolated point uses a rule and a street range to estimate a number's position. The [data and locales reference](../reference/data-and-locales.mdx) identifies the published candidate, point-of-interest, French, and United States bundles and the layers they declare. A bundle declaration doesn't establish that the data is installed or that a particular address has a matching row.

## The selected coordinate has a source and a grain

The published result extractor prefers an address-point match, then an interpolated point, then a street point, before falling back to a resolved administrative or postcode place. A matched venue can supply another point. `resolution_tier` records the mechanism that supplied the returned `lat` and `lon`. A parsed `house_number` can remain in the result when the coordinate comes from a postcode or locality. The text field and coordinate therefore answer different questions.

A source point is the coordinate stored on a matched place or address record. The returned coordinate can be that recorded point, a computed interpolation, a street or postcode point, or a representative administrative point. `coordinate_source` can preserve a matched address row's source and release, when that row has a source stamp. The published landmark branch can also record its landmark source on a venue result. `epistemic_status` states the claim supported by the selected evidence: for example, an authority-designated row and an observed row can both answer at `address_point` grain while carrying different statuses. The [result schema](../reference/schema.mdx#geocode-result-and-provenance) lists the fields and tiers.

An `uncertainty_m` value reports a radius supplied by the selected mechanism when available. Its value isn't an observed error for this input. A `null` radius means no radius was reported. Decimal places in `lat` and `lon` also do not establish the accuracy of the selected point. Accuracy requires a versioned evaluation against independently known positions for the relevant input population and data installation.

## Reverse geocoding asks about containment

Forward geocoding starts from text and chooses a point. Reverse geocoding starts from a supplied coordinate and asks which administrative place contains it. The published `WOFReverseGeocoder` searches bounding boxes in a WOF administrative database. When a polygon sidecar is available, it checks candidate polygons against the point. A bounding-box hit can include points outside a place's polygon. If no polygon confirms a place, the resolver can use its nearest-centroid fallback and report `containment: "approximate"`.

The reverse result reports a place hierarchy and whether the selected containment was polygon-confirmed or approximate. That relation concerns the input point and administrative geometry. It doesn't establish that a building or deliverable address exists at the input point. A finer boundary also cannot repair an incorrect input coordinate.
