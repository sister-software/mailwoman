Overnight I tried to teach the geocoder Canada. I pulled 843,000 Canadian postcodes, computed a centroid for every one, spot-checked the result (`M5H 2N2`, downtown Toronto, 43.652, −79.382, dead on) and validated the database every way I could read it: every postcode present, every coordinate right, and no other data disturbed. Every check passed. This morning, before flipping it live, I asked the demo to find a Toronto address, and it dropped the pin in Ohio.

How does a database that passes every check still land the answer about 370 kilometers wrong? What does it mean to "validate" a thing? And why is Toronto in Ohio?

## Building 843,000 Canadian postcode centroids

The task was simple enough: add Canada to the map. The obvious change is postcodes: they're precise, they're public, and Overture ships them. I'd done exactly this for a dozen European countries the night before, same tool, same shape: read the country's address points, group by postcode, take a trimmed centroid, write one row per code. Canada came back with 843,000 of them, alphanumeric `A1A 1A1` codes that survive the build intact, each pinned to its own block. I spot-checked Toronto's financial district and it sat exactly where it should. I diffed the new gazetteer against the old: US postcodes untouched, every European set intact, 843,000 new Canadian rows. All of those checks covered only what I'd built.

## The check that grades the pin

One test in the stack grades the _assembled_ answer rather than any single piece of the implementation. It loads the actual demo in a real browser, types an address into the box, and reads the coordinate off the map: the pin the user would see. Before promoting the new gazetteer I added a Canadian case to it, `100 Queen Street West, Toronto, ON M5H 2N2`, asserting that the pin lands somewhere in Toronto.

Four cases passed, Chicago, a Berlin postcode and the White House among them. The fifth came back 40.458, −80.6. I had to look it up. That's Toronto, Ohio: population five thousand, on the bank of the Ohio River, about 370 kilometers from the address I typed.

## The admin layer has no Canadian places

The gazetteer has two layers. There's the postcode layer I'd rebuilt, and underneath it the admin layer, the one that records a place's country and population. So I went and counted the admin layer: 1.7 million places, twenty-six countries. The number of Canadian ones is zero. Toronto, Montréal and Vancouver are all missing, because Canada isn't in it at all. We'd built the gazetteer out of the European set, the US, and the CJK countries, and no build step had ever folded Canada in.

The parser reads "Toronto" off the address and hands it to the resolver. The only Toronto the resolver has is the one in Ohio, so that's the city it picks. Then the cleverest piece of the cascade, the locality-first rule, finishes the job. We added that rule to keep Berlin's `10115`, which is also a perfectly good New York ZIP, from dragging German addresses to Manhattan: resolve the city first, then let that city restrict the postcode to its own country. It's a good rule, and here it backfires. The city resolved to the United States, so the correct Canadian postcode is now "from the wrong country," and the rule discards it. None of my 843,000 coordinates gets used, because the city is resolved first and the city is in Ohio.

## I validated the layer I made rather than the one that ships

I validated the postcodes, and they were correct. I never validated the thing the user receives: the coordinate, the pin, the assembled output of every layer working together. Each green check was accurate and beside the point, because none of them graded the coordinate the demo returns.

This keeps happening to us, in different forms. A model's per-tag F1 climbs while the coordinate it produces goes nowhere. A reconcile pass improves a label and breaks the geocode that depended on it. The discipline that survives every version of it is the same: grade the assembled coordinate against the truth rather than the component you happened to touch. The e2e check is the one check in the stack that reads the pin the user sees, and it's the only one that caught this.

## What the night's work is still good for

The night wasn't wasted. The 843,000 centroids are correct and sitting ready, and the tooling that made them is the same tooling that'll make the next country. But the premise was half a country short. A postcode resolves only once the country's admin layer, the cities and regions that anchor a name to a place, is in the gazetteer. Fold Canada's divisions into the gazetteer the way we folded Europe's, and the postcodes I built start resolving at no added cost. Shipped without that layer, they're never used.

So we didn't promote it, and the map still doesn't have Canada. Build the test that grades the pin your user sees, and run it before you trust the green checkmarks upstream of it.
