Our parser scores 28% on Spanish addresses. By the metric that reads its labels, it is, on paper, broken in a country of forty-seven million people. That metric grades labels, and the coordinate tells a different story.

We've [told this story once before](/research/2026/06/08/the-right-name-in-the-wrong-state), about a resolver that scored 93.7% on a name-match metric while landing 326 kilometers from the truth. We thought we'd learned the lesson (grade the coordinate rather than the word) and filed it under "things we now know." The same mistake caught us again, this time with Spanish addresses.

## A test that grades the words rather than the place

Here is the number that started it. We took 120 real Spanish addresses, pulled from OpenAddresses rather than invented, and ran them through the model, then scored its component labels against the truth: did it tag the street as `street`, the city as `locality`, and so on. Macro-F1 came back at **28.5%**. Street by itself: **24.9%**. By that yardstick, the model can barely read Spanish.

The question is what the number is counting. A Spanish street is `Calle Mayor` or `Avenida de la Constitución`: a type word, then a name. Our gold tags the whole span as the street, and the strict match wants the model's span to line up with it exactly. It mostly doesn't, and we wanted to know why: is the model splitting the type word off (calling `Calle` a prefix and `Mayor` the street, a harmless tagging-convention difference), or is it losing the street? So we re-graded against a bare-name gold, `Mayor` with the type word stripped from both sides. If the model were splitting `Calle` off, that should _reward_ it. It did the opposite: street-F1 fell from 25% to 2%. The model's span matches the whole `Calle Mayor` in far more rows than the bare name, so it's keeping the type word in rather than splitting it off. Its boundaries don't sit where our gold puts them, and the strict label match scores every such mismatch a flat zero, whether the address was understood or mangled.

## Coordinate error across eight locales

For a geocoder, the question that matters is whether the address landed in the right place on Earth. OpenAddresses ships a truth coordinate with every row, so we can ask it directly: parse the address, resolve it to a point, measure the great-circle distance to where it really is. We built held-out sets of 150 real addresses each across the locales where we have that coordinate truth, and graded the production model on the metric we ship.

We report **how frequently** the model produces a resolvable answer separately from **how good** that answer is when it does. Lump them together and a locale that fails half the time but nails the other half reads as mediocre everywhere.

| locale     | resolves | median error, when it resolves |
| ---------- | -------: | -----------------------------: |
| France     |      80% |                         1.3 km |
| Italy      |      79% |                         2.1 km |
| Luxembourg |      57% |                         0.3 km |
| Poland     |      53% |                         5.8 km |
| Portugal   |      52% |                         1.2 km |
| Austria    |      50% |                         5.2 km |
| Czechia    |      43% |                          44 km |
| Australia  |      28% |                         234 km |

Spain isn't in that table. Its open-address data is cadastral, with no clean coordinate to grade against, so for Spanish we can only score labels, and we can't put a Spanish address on the map in this test and measure it. What we _can_ do is take the exact street-boundary quirk that tanked the Spanish label score and see what it costs next door, in the Romance-language locales we can measure. There it costs no measurable coordinate accuracy. Where the model resolves an Italian or Portuguese address at all, it lands within a few kilometers, at city grain and sometimes on the rooftop. Luxembourg comes back at 300 meters. The model gets the locality and the postcode right frequently enough to find the right town while it draws the street boundary somewhere the label test penalizes. The 28% measured vocabulary rather than geocoding, and that holds in every Romance-language locale we have a coordinate to check it against. That's the strongest claim the data lets us make for Spanish without Spanish coordinates.

## The gap is how frequently it answers rather than how well

That's the good half. The other half is the left column, where the problem is.

Look down the `resolves` rate and a pattern falls out that's unrelated to accuracy and is entirely about us. France and Italy, the two locales we've poured training data into, resolve about 80% of the time. Everywhere we haven't, the rate drops by a third or more: Portugal, Poland, and Austria resolve around half the time, and Czechia resolves at 43. Australia resolves at 28 and misplaces what little it does resolve. We've barely touched Australia, and its own town names collide across states. The model isn't worse at _understanding_ those addresses. It's worse at producing an answer for them at all, and the cliff lines up exactly with where our corpus stops.

Precision isn't the limit: outside Czechia and Australia, when the model commits to an answer abroad, the median lands within six kilometers. One caveat: those are the answers the model _chose_ to give, and the addresses it drops skew toward the harder ones, so read the resolved distances as a ceiling rather than an average. The limit is recall, and recall tracks where our training data is.

## The towns are in the gazetteer

The version we'd have preferred is that the model parses everything correctly and the _gazetteer_ is missing half of Poland, so the fix is a data import that reflects badly on no one. We checked, and that version isn't true.

When a Portuguese address fails to resolve, it's not because the town is absent from our index. We folded the whole world's place names into the index months ago, and a spot check confirmed every Polish and Portuguese locality GeoNames knows is already there. It's that the model never emits the locality in the first place. The label test agrees, for once: Portuguese `locality`-recall sits at 39%, right alongside its 52% resolve rate. If the gazetteer were the bottleneck, the model would extract the town and the index would fail to find it. Instead the model and the resolver fail together: the towns are in the index, and the model doesn't reliably emit them as localities, so the resolver has no town to look up. We ran that check directly for Portuguese. Across the rest of the mid-tier the same shape repeats: low resolve, low locality-recall, and towns that the spot check found in the index. That pattern is what you'd expect if the cause is the same one.

No clever rule or `Calle`-handling special case fixes this. Bolt on a Spanish street splitter and you've taught the parser one convention by hand and learned no general rule for the next forty. The model fails on these addresses because it hasn't seen enough of the place, so the change is the training distribution, and we now have a coordinate-graded yardstick that will tell us, locale by locale, whether feeding it more moves the recall it's supposed to.

## Conclusions

The lesson is the same one as last time. We had it written down, and the label metric still nearly led us to the wrong conclusion before the coordinate corrected it. Distance to the truth doesn't depend on matching the gold's words or on which language the street type is in, so we grade on it. Every other metric is a proxy, and the proxies we use score us higher in the languages we trained on and lower in the ones we didn't.

The two columns also show that a model can be _right_ and _unavailable_ at the same time, and if you only measure one number you can't tell which problem you have. Measure recall separately from precision before you decide what's broken. Ours is accurate where it answers in six of the eight locales we measured, and the next work is getting it to answer for more addresses, in more places, by showing it more of the world.
