Two days ago we retired a decode-time workaround we'd been leaning on, and that exposed
something it had been hiding: our parser can't read a French address backwards. Last night
we tried to teach it to. We got most of the way, stalled at 87%, and did the obvious thing
and turned the training signal up, fully expecting the last few points to follow. The model
got worse instead, and this post explains how.

## French house numbers after the span bridge was retired

A French address can lead with its postcode: `47110 Sainte-Livrade-sur-Lot, 69 Allée du
Bugatel`. The house number, `69`, sits in the back half. Our model learned house numbers
from data that almost always puts them out front, so when the number moves, the model
labels the first number-shaped thing it sees, `47110`, the postcode, as the house number.

We hadn't noticed, because a decode-time span bridge was papering over the
worst of it. When we shipped the char-offset corpus and retired that bridge (by then a
confirmed no-op everywhere else), French house numbers were the one tag that
fell out the bottom: 97.7 down to 89.6. That's the regression we set out to fix.

## Eighty-seven percent against a bar of 91

The fix looked obvious. German addresses have the same any-order problem, and we'd
already beaten it with a synthetic extract that teaches both word orders. So we built the
French version: real street and city data, half the rows flipped into the postcode-first
shapes the model keeps tripping on, every house number labeled correctly wherever it
landed. Then we retrained and re-measured against a French eval we'd widened from
one town to fifty-six.

It worked, mostly. On that harder eval the shipped model scores 54.5%. It's blind to
every reordered row. The retrain came back at **87.4%**. A thirty-three point swing from a
single extract is the kind of number that makes you want to ship before coffee.

Except the bar was 91, and 87.4 isn't 91. Every miss was the same shape, too: `47110
Sainte-Livrade-sur-Lot, 69 …` with `47110` labeled as the house number. The model had
learned the back-half number existed. It hadn't learned to stop grabbing the
postcode out front. The signal was there and slightly too quiet.

## So we turned it up

If an extract at weight 3 gets you to 87, an extract at weight 6 gets you the rest of the way.
That was the bet, and it's the same weight the German extract runs at, so we had
precedent. We doubled it and retrained.

It came back at **84.7%**, below the weight-3 run. When we dumped the misses, the model wasn't
making the old mistake more frequently. It had invented a new one. On `47110
Sainte-Livrade-sur-Lot, 69 Allée du Bugatel`, instead of grabbing the whole postcode, it
now split `47110` into a house number `4` and a postcode `7110`. Sometimes it glued the
postcode and the real house number into a single number, `47110 85`. At weight 6 the model
had started splitting a house number out of the postcode itself. The postcode tag had been
rock-solid, and it slipped too.

The German precedent didn't transfer. A German house number is always _last_, so there's
one position to find, and more practice sharpens the aim. A postcode-first French
address puts a five-digit number and a one-to-four-digit number in the same neighborhood,
and the only thing telling them apart is which is which. A higher weight on "look for a
number up front" amplifies that ambiguity instead of resolving it, up to the point where
the model splits a postcode in half to find a house number.

## Why more weight made it worse

Two days ago we wrote that you can't fix a representation gap with more data. A
data-realism gap doesn't close when you turn up the weight either. The synthetic extract
taught the model a caricature of reordered French: useful enough for a thirty-three point
jump, too coarse to close the last stretch, and actively harmful past a point, because more
exposure deepened the caricature. The plateau isn't a quantity
problem we can outspend. The model needs reordered examples that look like the real thing,
or a signal that guards the postcode from being raided, and neither of those is a
bigger number in a config file.

So we held. The 87% model is a solid gain over what's live, and we'll likely put
it in front of people once we decide whether a number that beats the old one but
misses its own bar is enough to ship. The recovery itself waits for the right change, and
this change isn't it: two training runs and about ten dollars of GPU showed that raising
the weight doesn't close the gap, and the fragmented postcodes in the weight-6 misses are
the evidence.
