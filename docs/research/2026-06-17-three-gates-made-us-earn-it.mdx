Our failure taxonomy now ranks boundary instability first among the parser's weaknesses. We
spent a night building training data to fix it and then chose not to retrain. Three separate
checks each caught a step that looked reasonable and was wrong, and this post is about those
checks more than the extract. The checks let us work quickly on the worst weakness without
shipping a fix that improves the headline number while introducing hidden errors.

## The weakness with several faces

Boundary instability means that where two address components meet, the model puts the boundary
in the wrong place. For `Country Club Rd`, the street name absorbs its own suffix. For
`North Sydney NSW 2060` with the commas stripped, the city and the state merge. For a French
street like `Neuve-des-Capucines 5`, the house number is absorbed into the name.

For a while we filed several of these cases under "within-token punctuation" and blamed the
apostrophes and hyphens. When we measured that class, the punctuation was almost never the
cause. `O'Connell` parses fine, and so does `Coeur d'Alene`. What breaks is the boundary next to
the punctuation. The same boundary error appears in many different forms.

## The plan

We planned a synthetic extract that places the gold boundary exactly where the model errs,
spread across enough real-looking addresses that a retrain learns the boundary from context
instead of memorizing the strings. The steps were to generate it, baseline the current model to
measure the size of the gap, write the recipe, and add it to the corpus.

## Check one: the extract that graded on a curve

The first version drew from a thin vocabulary of sixteen street names and a handful of cities.
Twenty thousand rows from sixteen streets means the model sees `Country Club Rd` a thousand
times, so it learns the word and not the boundary. We expected that. We did not expect the
baseline result: the current model scored 48% on the suffix boundary and 70% on the French
prefix against that thin data, which looked reasonable.

We then tripled the vocabulary to a hundred streets and real cities, with every row unique, and
the same model scored 41 and 48. The thin data had two effects. It taught the word instead of
the boundary, and it inflated the baseline score. The runbook already warns that thin diversity
teaches memorization instead of the boundary. The warning now has a measurement next to it.

## Check two: the postcode that was already a house number

To teach the Australian slash convention, where `4/2A` means unit 4, number 2A, the extract used
Australian addresses. The base-consistency lint blocked it. In our training corpus the token
`3000` appears thousands of times, always as a US house number, and the extract labeled it as an
Australian postcode. A coverage extract cannot outweigh the base corpus it is added to. It can
only conflict with the base corpus and degrade results slowly over one to three releases, which
happened to us before. The lint caught the conflict before we spent any GPU-hours on it. We
removed Australia, the extract now covers base locales only, and the slash convention became a
separate task that must also add base coverage for its locale.

## Check three: the city that was typically a street

With the extract limited to the locales we train on, the lint found another problem. In our
corpus, tokens like Paris, Springfield, and Burlington are mostly streets (Paris Avenue,
Springfield Street) and only rarely cities. Our extract labels them as localities. Either label
can be correct depending on context, and the model may be context-aware enough to handle it. The
lint does not assume that. It flagged the minority sense, because the last time we let a similar
case through, a theater on 5th Avenue caused an extract to label `5th Avenue` as a venue. The
retrain will track this number, and a cleaner extract will draw its cities from names that the corpus
already labels as cities.

## Why we didn't retrain

At the end of the night, the extract was built and the baseline measured. The recipe was written
and approved, the corpus assembled, and the manifest tested against the real base. Every
checklist item was complete, and a training run needed one more command. We deliberately did not
run it.

The three checks were the main work of the night. The taxonomy identified the weakness. The
checks blocked the fix three times, once for the data and twice for the corpus. Each block cost
a few minutes and prevented a retrain whose headline numbers would have looked fine while its
labels were wrong. **Letting the checks block a fix until it passes them is the fastest way we
know to fix a serious weakness correctly.**
