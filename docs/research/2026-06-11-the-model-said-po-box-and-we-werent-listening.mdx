Last night our release check failed twice before it passed, and both failures had the
same cause: the decoder discarded information the model had produced. When a model fails
an eval, the usual responses are more data or more parameters. This post is about a third
option. It covers three questions: why a model that scored 89 on post-office boxes in
validation scored 60 at the check, why fixing that broke French postcodes, and what this
says about where a parser's knowledge lives.

## A tag with a 98% failure rate, sorted by punctuation

Our parser had three empty rows on its scorecard: `po_box`, `cedex`, and intersections.
Last night's release candidate carried new training data for all three, and the check had
pre-registered floors for each. Intersections scored 100 and cedex scored 96. `po_box`
scored 60.4 against a floor of 70.

We expected the failure to come from a thin layout class or an under-weighted extract.
Instead, the failures sorted by a key we had never graded by: punctuation. Plain leaders
like `PO Box 19` passed at 84%. Dotted leaders (`P.O. Box`, `C.P.`, `B.P.`) failed at
**98%**, and every failure was a truncation at the first period. The model would emit
`p` and stop.

We dumped the raw per-piece labels for `P.O. BOX 3274`, and the model had labeled _every
piece correctly_: `P` as `po_box` at 0.93 confidence, `O` as `po_box` at 0.94, and
`BOX 3274` as `po_box` at 0.96. The span broke apart at the periods. Our corpus format
tokenizes on whitespace and drops standalone punctuation, so it cannot put a label _on a
dot_. None of the 670 million training rows could teach the span to stay whole, and ten
times more training data had moved the score by 2.9 points. More repetition cannot fix a
gap in the representation.

## Listening is a decoder feature

The fix needed neither new data nor new weights. It is a dozen lines of decode logic that
we now call the span bridge. When two fragments carry the same tag and only a short run of
intra-token punctuation (dots, hyphens, or slashes) separates them, the bridge merges them
and absorbs the punctuation the corpus could never label. The model and weights stayed the
same, with no GPU time spent. `po_box` went from 60.4 to 87.

The re-check then failed on French postcodes, and this failure taught us more. The bridge
had also been merging across commas. For `Sainte-Livrade-sur-Lot 47110, 9016 Route de Lablasie`,
the model sometimes labeled the house number as a second postcode, and the comma was the
only thing keeping the two spans separate. The bridge merged the mistake into
`47110, 9016`. A period sits _inside_ a surface form, and a comma separates two of them.
Once the bridge distinguished the two, French postcodes recovered and `po_box` rose another
two points.

The third battery passed 17 floors out of 17, and the release shipped before sunrise.

## Where the knowledge lives

We have learned this more than once. The transformer is one layer of a system, and an
eval failure points at the _system_, which may or may not mean the weights. Last month a
stubborn tag turned out to be contradictory labels in the corpus. Last night it was a
label format that could not represent what the model already knew. In both cases, scaling
the model or the data would have cost a week and changed nothing, because the constraint
was in a different layer. Reading forty failing rows found it.

Finding it this way cost three full check batteries in one night and a pre-registered
floor that we did not round up. That cost is worth paying. A check that fails twice and
shows why is more useful than several that pass without explanation.

A larger fix is still needed. The punctuation gap is a property of our corpus format, and
the proper fix (labels addressed by character offset rather than token index) is a bigger
change than one night allows. The bridge is a documented workaround. The scorecard that
had three empty rows yesterday has none today, and the parser now outputs "P.O. Box" as
one span, matching the per-piece labels the model had produced all along.

Check what your decoder discards.
