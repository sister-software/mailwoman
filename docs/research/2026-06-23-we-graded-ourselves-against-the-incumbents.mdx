Our geocoder had beaten Nominatim across the United States by fifteen points, so we ran the same benchmark on Europe and expected a similar result. Europe produced a double-digit loss.

Losing to Nominatim was acceptable, because Nominatim covers the whole planet on community-contributed data and is the standard to beat. The problem was that we could not explain the loss. We knew our parser was not ten points worse in Europe than in America, so we investigated what the gap consisted of.

This post covers the cause, the fixes, and the problems the fixes did not solve. For the European comparison we added a third system: Pelias, through geocode.earth, the hosted Elasticsearch stack many teams use. Pelias turned out to be the stronger competitor, and this post states where it still beats us.

## How we kept score

We graded on where the address lands on Earth, which is how we argue geocoders should be graded, and ignored whether the model's labels matched ours. OpenAddresses ships a truth coordinate with every row. For each address we parse it, resolve it to a point, and measure the great-circle distance to the true location. A result within 25 kilometers counts as correctly placed, and a no-result counts as a miss, the same as a wrong answer. Each system gets one number on the same real addresses, and each gets the same country hint.

This standard is stricter than the one we had been using internally. Our internal "resolve rate" counted any resolution as a success, including results that landed a region away. Re-graded at 25 km, the internal metric had overstated our European results by fifteen to twenty points. The benchmark's first effect was to correct our own scoreboard, and we now grade the coordinate.

## The US, briefly — because it's why Europe stung

In the US, mailwoman correctly places **99%** of these addresses at 25 km, and Nominatim places **84%**. Almost all of the difference is _no-result_: ours is near zero, and Nominatim's is about one in six. That difference reflects our purpose-built data, with TIGER and a fifty-state rooftop layer covering areas where community OSM data is still incomplete.

We get that result from a 30 MB model that runs in your browser, without Elasticsearch or a server round-trip. The same engine, deployed the same way, ran on Europe. That is why a double-digit loss there was confusing.

## Europe: we weren't wrong, we were silent

Graded the same way across six European locales, mailwoman started at about **66%**. Nominatim scored **78%**, and Pelias, which uses Elasticsearch and a mix of sources, scored **89%**. We were behind both. The obvious explanation is that the parser is worse in Europe, but the data did not support it. When mailwoman _does_ resolve a European address, the point is within a kilometer or two of the truth. Almost the whole gap came from the other failure mode: more than one in four messy European addresses came back **empty**. The model did not place them wrong. It returned no place at all.

Empty results are easier to fix than wrong ones. We investigated why the results were empty and found two separate causes.

## Why it went quiet, part one: a word it mispronounced

The first cause is fragmentation. The subword tokenizer splits a town like **Grudziądz** at the accented `ą` into `Grudzi` + `dz`. Neither half is a place, so neither resolves, and the whole address fails. The full town name was present in the input the entire time. The tokenizer split a word that it had received intact.

The unresolved results contain many cases like this: Polish, Czech, and Portuguese towns that the model split at a diacritic and then could not find.

## Why it went quiet, part two: postcodes we never had

The second cause was missing data. We had no postcodes for Poland and Czechia. WhosOnFirst, our gazetteer's main source, has rich postcode coverage for some countries and none for others, and PL and CZ had zero rows. A Polish address whose town the model _did_ read correctly could still fail, because the postcode it could have resolved by was absent from the database.

## The fixes: read the word, and fill the gap

For the first cause, when a parse resolves nothing, the resolver now returns to the raw text, using the actual characters with diacritics intact instead of the model's subwords. It enumerates the whitespace tokens, matches each against the same-country gazetteer, and takes the longest exact match, because the real town is usually the longer, specific name and not an ambiguous prefix. Before trusting it, we checked that it recovered _real coordinates_. The recovered towns land about 1.8 km from the truth, and not in a same-named place elsewhere in the country.

For the second cause, we imported the missing postcodes from GeoNames: about 73,000 for Poland and 15,000 for Czechia, each with its own centroid and an open license. We added them directly to the gazetteer without a model change or retrain.

We did not expect the next result. **The two fixes mostly solve the same cases by different routes.** Where we now have the postcode, the address resolves directly and word recovery has nothing to do. Where we lack it, recovery resolves the address. Graded the same way, the postcode fill alone raised Europe from 66% to 84%. Word recovery alone also raised it from 66% to 84%. Together they reached **88%**, and the empty-result rate fell from more than one in four to under one in twelve.

## The third fix: stop being confidently wrong

At 88%, a set of misses remained, and these were wrong results instead of empty ones. "06260 Saint-Pierre, La Placette 7" came back **617 kilometers** from the truth. Saint-Pierre is one of the most common town names in France, and we had picked the wrong one. The postcode, 06260, had resolved correctly to the Alpes-Maritimes, where the address is. The coordinate picker trusted the town name and never checked it against the postcode in the same parse, so it discarded the correct answer.

We added that check. When a town lands far from its own postcode, the picker chooses the same-named town nearest the postcode instead. If none is close, it falls back to the postcode's own point. This applies the consistency check from recovery to the main path, without a model change. It raised Europe from 88% to **94%**. After we extended the GeoNames postcode fill to Portugal, Austria, and Australia, it also raised Australia from 35% to 65%, because a resolved postcode gave the disambiguation a reference point.

## The direct part

We have been wrong before by declaring success early, so here are the numbers without interpretation. Graded the same way on the European set, all three systems side by side: mailwoman **94%**, Nominatim **78%**, and Pelias **89%**. We now lead both, by five points over a hosted Elasticsearch stack and sixteen over Nominatim, with a 30 MB model running in a browser tab. We lead outright in Italy, Portugal, France, and Czechia.

Across all seven locales including Australia, the results are **90% for us, 81% Nominatim, and 88% Pelias**. We still lead, but the margin is thin, and Australia accounts for all of the narrowing: we score **65%** there to Nominatim's 97. We have now worked out that Australia is a _parsing_ problem. Australian addresses in this set lead with the postcode ("3053 Carlton, Barry Street 50"). Our model, trained on American and European formats where the number comes first, reads the leading 3053 as a house number and mislabels everything after it. Given the **same address reordered** in the American format, it parses correctly, and 65% becomes 87%. The fix belongs in the model: we will train it on the Australian format using the national address file. Australia remains unfixed. We know the cause, and it lies in the parser. The reordered addresses resolve with the existing gazetteer, so the gazetteer needs no change.

There is also a precision cost. At 25 km we're competitive, and at one kilometer we are not. We resolve to admin and postcode **centroids**, so we place the _area_ correctly, within a couple of kilometers, where Nominatim and Pelias place a rooftop pin. The headline result therefore means the right neighborhood and not the right building. For many jobs the neighborhood is enough, and where it is not, we say so.

Word recovery stays **off by default**, because about one in six of the _newly_ recovered addresses lands more than 25 km away when recovery matched the wrong same-named place. Where we have postcode coverage, a consistency check rejects those before they are returned. The GeoNames fill means that check now runs for Poland and Czechia, where a month ago it could not. Australia is where it still cannot run. A batch pipeline therefore leaves recovery off, because a wrong coordinate corrupts a join without any visible error. The demo turns recovery on, because in an interactive tool a visible, correctable result is more useful than no result. Every recovered place is tagged with whether the postcode check ran, so each caller can decide how to treat it.

## The thing a scoreboard can't show

A single resolve rate also leaves out confidence. A search index returns a result without saying how much to trust it. Mailwoman's confidence is _calibrated_: a span it marks 90% is correct about 90% of the time, measured on held-out data. You can build a rule on that number, such as auto-accepting above a threshold and routing the rest to a human reviewer, and the threshold behaves as you set it. The recovered-but-unverified coordinates above are flagged separately and are not reported as calibrated probabilities, because the calibrated number is only useful if it stays accurate.

Here is where we stand. Pelias is ahead of us across the full panel, and it places rooftops where we place neighborhoods. In Europe we beat Nominatim by a wide margin and matched Pelias, using 30 megabytes in a browser, without Elasticsearch, and with a calibrated confidence number. We still trail on parts of the map, and Australia is unsolved. We mostly closed a double-digit gap by reading town names that the tokenizer had been splitting and by adding postcodes we should have shipped from the start. We will keep grading on the coordinate.
